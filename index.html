Diana

Side-scrolling delivery roguelite · Solo project · In development

  • Godot 4.6
  • GDScript
  • ~2,700 LOC

You fly cargo down a hostile lane for a dispatch office that will not tell you what is in the crates. The twist is that surviving the run and delivering well are two different skills — and the game pays you for the second one.

The loop

Take a contract → fly the lane → get graded on arrival → spend seeds at the depot → slot new modules → take a harder contract. Fourteen contracts unlock one at a time, each trading better pay against faster fuel burn and a longer lane.

Design decisions I’d defend in an interview

LEVEL DESIGN
Authored chunks, not random spawners

The lane emits hand-built obstacle patterns one at a time on distance, rather than firing independent random spawners on timers. That is what gives a run a readable rhythm instead of uniform noise. Coins are the pathing language — they trace the line you are meant to fly, or bait you into a worse one. They are never scattered at random.

PACING
Forced breathers

Every third chunk is guaranteed to be difficulty zero — no hazards at all. Pressure needs somewhere to release, otherwise a long run reads as one flat wall of stress and players stop registering the spikes.

ECONOMY
One letter tells the whole story

Cargo degrades from hazard hits, hard bounces and sustained high-G flying. The delivery grade (S–F) drives both the payout share and the reputation gained — so the letter on the results screen is the entire outcome, not a label sitting next to a separate calculation. Coins are deliberately excluded from grade scaling: a battered run still keeps what it picked up.

PROGRESSION
Slot scarcity forces identity

Thirty-one permanent modules across eight tags — but only two bays to start, five at most, with extra bays priced steeply. You cannot own the board. Scarcity is what turns a shopping list into a build.

SHOP
Five offers, not a catalogue

The depot shows a fixed five offers that rotate after each run, with a rising reroll cost. You meet the catalogue a few items at a time, so buying one is a real choice under uncertainty rather than optimisation against a full menu.

DIFFICULTY
A curve with no plateau

Scroll speed ramps fast, eases into a shoulder that keeps the opening readable, then creeps upward forever. Every run ends because the lane eventually outruns you — there is no speed you can settle into.

UI
One colour language everywhere

Blue always means one-use, purple always means permanent — in the shop, in the loadout screen and on the in-flight hotbar. The hotbar keys live in a single constant so three separate screens can never disagree about which key fires which slot.

FEEDBACK
Modules that show their work

Each slotted module gets a chip in the HUD, dim until it actually does something. Always-on modules stay lit; triggered ones flash on fire. You can see your build working instead of trusting a tooltip.

Entrelaços

A calm, culturally-native puzzle game for Brazil · Product Designer (solo) · 1 month

  • Figma
  • Google AI Studio
  • Google Play

Brazil is the largest mobile gaming market in Latin America and an Android-first country — over 80% platform share. Yet its players (70% self-identified “casual”) are stuck between culturally distant NYT-style puzzles and loud, ad-stuffed clones machine-translated into generic PT-PT. I saw a gap: a quiet, locally-fluent word game built for the micro-moment — the two minutes in a queue, the morning coffee — as players pivot away from doomscrolling toward calmer play.

The craft — a “low-dopamine” design language

Every visual decision was made to be the opposite of slot-machine gaming UI. I chose neobrutalist minimalism: high contrast, low saturation, raw borders, honest typography. No glossy bubbles, no neon, no confetti. The interface gets out of the way so the only thing lighting up is the player’s own logic.

Killing the wireframe

Instead of weeks in Figma auto-layout, I prototyped functionally with AI-assisted coding in Google AI Studio. Logic games live or die on feel, so I needed to play them, not look at them — testing whether a “snap” felt rewarding, whether the haptics were too aggressive for a calm game, and hand-tuning difficulty across 21 levels to find the flow state between boredom and anxiety.

Testing that changed the product

Five Brazilian puzzle players, tracked for emotional resonance rather than pass/fail:

CATEGORIES
“Americanized” references

Rebuilt around Brazilian slang, regional food and pop culture to trigger real aha moments.

DIFFICULTY
Early levels felt mindless

Solved too fast — recalibrated toward non-obvious connections.

FLOW
Stuck players got frustrated

Added a shame-free Reveal escape hatch to protect flow, with no “Game Over” wall.

ONBOARDING
The UI hid its own entry point

A silent onboarding using motion cues instead of text.

Motion & the web build

The game is playable directly in the browser — I designed the web experience around it, so anyone can start in one click with no install. Motion does real work throughout: a satisfying snap when four words lock into a group, smooth reveal transitions as each category resolves, and soft feedback states for near-misses and wrong guesses — tuned to reward the solve without overstimulating, true to the low-dopamine goal.

Outcome & north star

Live on Google Play (publisher: Myaza) and playable on the web. The core success signal is session completion rate — is the game becoming a quiet daily ritual rather than another notification? This project marks how I work now: when AI turns the how of building into a commodity, the designer’s value moves to the what and the why — the feel of the interaction and respect for the player’s time.

Pixel Party

Looking-for-players, without the toxicity · Product Design · 2 months

  • Figma

Finding people to play with online is easy; finding the right people isn’t — research shows over 80% of matches carry verbal abuse (Polygon). Pixel Party is a cross-platform LFP tool that lets gamers spin up a session — casual co-op, ranked, or an in-person board-game night — in minutes, without the endless group-chat back-and-forth.

Visual language

I pulled cues straight from gaming interfaces: bold colour contrast, clean typography for fast readability, and subtle motion to make the flow feel alive. Microcopy was rewritten to be short, active and game-friendly — the product should feel like starting a match, not filling out a form.

The flow, stripped to its core

Rapid low-fidelity wireframes, then live user testing, then high-fidelity. Watching players interact pointed hard at one thing: speed.

  • Event creation in just a few steps.
  • Game and platform selection that feels instant — pre-filled suggestions, meeting communities where they already are (Discord, Reddit).
  • One-tap RSVP, no app download, with an optional “Add to Calendar.”

Personas that anchored the decisions

The Competitive Connector, the Social Explorer and the Event Host — each with a different tolerance for friction, which is exactly what a polished onboarding had to absorb.

Outcome

A validated high-fidelity concept with a clear success model — DAU/MAU stickiness, event engagement (reactions, replies, RSVPs), and polls created. The visual and interaction system leaves a clean runway for tournaments and recurring events.

Cloudya Onboarding

Teaching a dense product to feel effortless, through motion · Product Designer · NFON (Cloudya)

  • Figma (Smart Animate)
  • Lottie
  • Rive

Cloudya is a feature-dense B2B communications product, and first-run is where dense products lose people. New users landed in a powerful but unfamiliar interface with too much to absorb at once. I designed an onboarding system that guides people to their first meaningful action — using motion to carry attention instead of walls of tooltip text.

Motion as guidance, not decoration

  • Built lightweight, performant animations in Lottie and Rive that ship to production — motion that runs live in the real product, not just a prototype reel.
  • Prototyped and timed interactions in Figma Smart Animate to nail pacing and easing before developer handoff.
  • Used staged reveals that direct the eye one step at a time, cutting cognitive load and letting users learn by doing rather than reading.

Impact

Reduced first-run confusion and made the product feel approachable from the first screen.

Why it matters here

This is production motion design — the same craft behind good landing-page and campaign micro-interactions — tuned for clarity and performance, not just polish.

Nia

An AI assistant, designed to feel human · Product Designer · NFON (Cloudya) · 3 months

  • Figma
  • DaisyUI

NFON’s move toward AI started with a customer-facing chatbot: answer product and sales questions in minutes, not hours. I mapped the support journey to find where fragmented information and repetitive questions cost the most time — then designed Nia to be the confident frontline, embedded across the web app and site.

Design & craft decisions

SYSTEM
One library, native feel everywhere

With a developer, I built a single component library on DaisyUI, with native iOS adjustments so the widget felt at home on every surface.

VOICE
Tone as a design material

Nia started robotic — “Sorry, I can’t answer that,” a dead end. Loosening the guardrails toward a warmer, more natural voice measurably lifted satisfaction scores.

RECOVERY
No dead ends

Recovery paths — clarifying questions, docs, human escalation — so the conversation always had a next step.

CLARITY
Visual guidance

Step-by-step instructions paired with visuals on complex answers.

TIMING
Feedback, timed right

NPS triggered after three messages — enough for the user to have an opinion, early enough not to interrupt.

Rollout & impact

We launched internally on Confluence first — one of the fastest releases in NFON’s history — to validate flows and surface missing documentation before going public. “Have you asked Nia?” became the office reflex for any question. The result: measurable ticket deflection, resolution time cut from days to minutes, and internal and external docs that improved directly from real usage patterns.

Unified Design System

Craft at scale · UI Components · 6 months

A token-based design system unifying a large legacy ecosystem across web, iOS, Android, and a desktop admin portal — kicked off in a hackathon with a design-to-AI-to-code workflow.

I audited component libraries across every platform, standardised structures alongside developers, and built a deliberately platform-conscious system — not one-size-fits-all — around four principles: Innovative, Helpful, Seamless, Focused. A partial rollout on one product line delivered improved visual consistency, faster development cycles from reusable components, and a shared language between design and engineering. Honest about scope: not yet company-wide, and Storybook coverage was incomplete — but it proved the system could adapt under changing constraints.
